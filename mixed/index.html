ENERGY & ECONOMICS2 MIN READEnglish + 中文

Why efficiency can make us use more.

Imagine replacing an old car with one that travels farther on the same amount of fuel. Each trip now costs less. So you take the scenic route, visit friends more often, or choose to drive instead of taking the train.

That change in behavior is an example of the rebound effect（反弹效应）. When a service becomes cheaper to use, we may use more of it, offsetting some of the savings we expected. The technology improved. Our habits moved too.

An especially strong version of this idea is associated with Jevons paradox（杰文斯悖论）. More efficient steam engines did not simply mean less coal: they helped make steam power useful in more places. But not every rebound cancels all the savings, and efficiency does not inevitably increase total consumption.

The useful question is not whether energy efficiency（能源效率） matters. It is what happens after the improvement. A better engine changes a machine. A cheaper journey can change a decision.

PSYCHOLOGY & MONEY2 MIN READEnglish + 中文

How much difference can one cent make?

Two price tags sit beside each other: $2.99 and $3.00. The gap is one cent. Yet the first price can feel meaningfully smaller. Your arithmetic has not changed. The way your attention meets the number has.

Researchers call this the left-digit effect（左位数字效应）. When the leading digit changes, a tiny numerical difference can produce a larger difference in perceived size. The effect depends on how the prices are compared; it is not a rule that every price ending in nine wins.

The comparison point matters, too. A reference price（参考价格） is the benchmark you bring to a purchase: perhaps yesterday’s price, a competing product, or what you expected to pay. The same tag can look expensive or cheap against a different benchmark.

This is a small window into numerical cognition（数字认知）: how we perceive and work with numbers. A price is a quantity, but it is also something a human has to read. Sometimes, the interesting part begins before the calculation.

DECISIONS & BEHAVIOR2 MIN READEnglish + 中文

When the menu gets longer, choosing gets harder.

You open a menu looking for lunch. Five minutes later, you are still reading. There are more dishes you might like, but also more comparisons to make. Having options and knowing what to choose are different things.

This is a setting where choice overload（选择过载） can appear: adding options can make a decision harder rather than more satisfying. But it is not a universal law. Research finds that the situation and the person making the choice matter.

One factor is choice-set complexity（选项集复杂性）. Options that differ on many dimensions can be difficult to compare, especially under time pressure. Another is preference uncertainty（偏好不确定性）: if you do not yet know what matters most to you, a longer list may not help you find it.

A familiar favorite might make a large menu easy to navigate. An unfamiliar category might do the opposite. The more useful question is not “How many options is too many?” but “What would help me compare the options I have?”

A few things to know

Do I need an API key?

No paid API key is required for this preview. Explained uses the DeepSeek website in your browser. You may need to sign in, and DeepSeek’s own usage limits apply.

What gets shared?

With lookups enabled, your selected phrase and optionally up to 600 nearby characters are sent to DeepSeek. You can inspect the context, turn it off, and pause lookups. DeepSeek may retain the chats.

Where does it work?

Normal HTML reading pages in Chrome 114 or later. PDFs, browser settings pages and document editors are outside this preview’s scope.
